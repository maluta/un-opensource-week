### **Event Overview**

*   **Event:** OSPO for Good, part of the UN's Open Source Week.
*   **Host:** UN Office of the Secretary-General's Envoy on Technology.
*   **Objective:** To explore how Open Source Program Offices (OSPOs) institutionalize open source across government, academia, and industry; and to discuss its critical intersection with AI, open data, and Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI).

---

### **1. Opening Remarks & Vision Setting**

The day was opened by organizers, followed by a keynote address setting the stage for the week's discussions.

#### **Amandeep Singh Gill - UN Under-Secretary-General and Special Envoy**
*   **Core Message:** The open source community has grown from a small group of enthusiasts into a powerful, multi-stakeholder force for global digital cooperation. Losing control to the community is a good thing; it fosters serendipity and connection.
*   **Key Achievements Highlighted:**
    *   **UN Takeover:** An action-packed hackathon, maintain-a-thon, and edit-a-thon that saw 81 hackers tackle UNICEF challenges and volunteers generate nearly 2 million article views.
    *   **UN Progress:** The UN has released its own open-source repository, catalog, policy framework, and principles, demonstrating a commitment to "walking the talk."
*   **Critical Theme:** The UN is not just for governments; it is a space for all stakeholders. The Global Digital Compact explicitly recognizes open source as a foundation for digital transformation.

---

### **2. Panel: "A Look Behind Newly Launched OSPOs"**

This panel explored the diverse motivations and challenges of establishing OSPOs across different sectors.

*   **Saeed Choudhury (Academia - Carnegie Mellon):** Stressed that university OSPOs succeed by linking their mission directly to the institution's core goals of research, education, and social impact. The key is to change the culture systematically, not through abrupt declarations.
*   **Nithya Ruff (Private Sector - Amazon):** Described the OSPO journey from an initial focus on legal compliance to a mature, strategic function for community engagement. The OSPO acts as a "bridge builder," translating between internal business language and external open-source culture.
*   **Bastien Guerri (Government - France):** Explained that France's OSPO was created in response to calls from civil society to institutionalize efforts beyond a few "hacktivists." A key challenge is balancing the need for short-term, visible impact with long-term investment in maintenance and community.
*   **Richard Gevers (Government - South Africa):** Highlighted that their OSPO was born from a "crisis of trust" after the pandemic. Its core motivations are sovereignty, trust, and inclusion. The OSPO's role is not to serve one user perfectly, but to "hold a contested space" for diverse citizen needs.
*   **Avni Katri (Private Sector - GitHub):** Explained that GitHub's OSPO was formalized post-acquisition to mature its practices and "follow the best practices that we espouse." The OSPO helps the organization serve as a source of knowledge and encourage global collaboration.

---

### **3. Breakout Session Share-Outs**

Organizers reported back on key discussions from the morning's interactive breakout sessions.

| Breakout Session Topic | Key Discussion Points | Key Takeaway |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Open Source & Humanitarian** | Sustainability is the biggest challenge. There is funding for pilots and polished solutions, but a "valley of death" for the messy middle where projects often fail. | Free as in "free puppy" still applies; HFOSS (Humanitarian Free and Open Source Software) has real costs for infrastructure, adoption, and building trust. |
| **2. Effective Governmental OSPOs** | Pilots in Kenya and Trinidad & Tobago show that OSPOs must be built in collaboration with local academic and tech ecosystems. Long-term financial viability is critical. | A new **Public Sector OSPO Network** (`floss-pso.network`) was announced to help public sector OSPOs connect and learn from each other. |
| **3. InnerSourcing** | InnerSourcing applies open-source models *within* an organization. The greatest challenges are not technical but cultural: changing mindsets, overcoming legal hurdles, and getting buy-in from middle management. | Success requires a dual top-down (C-level support) and bottom-up (developer buy-in) approach, starting with experiments and clear policies. |

---

### **4. Thematic Deep Dives: AI, Data, and DPI**

The afternoon sessions explored the application of open-source principles to major technological shifts.

*   **Keynote Video (Yann LeCun, Meta):**
    *   **Core Message:** AI is not a zero-sum game. Open source democratizes AI, accelerates progress, and improves transparency and accountability.
    *   **Vision:** Future AI systems will be a repository of all human knowledge, which requires global, open collaboration to build, as no single entity can capture all languages and cultures.

*   **Panels on AI, Data, and DPI:**
    *   **AI & Ethics:** A recurring theme was that we don't need "new values" for AI; we need to protect the human rights and democratic principles we have already built. Open source is a key mechanism for ensuring AI serves humanity, not just a few powerful actors.
    *   **Open Data:** The conversation shifted from viewing data as an "asset" or "oil" to stewarding it as a "commons." Data is useless without the governance, standards, and community to make it discoverable and trustworthy.
    *   **Open Source in DPI:** Panelists used the analogy of airports (public infrastructure) vs. airlines (private innovation). Open source provides the foundational, cooperative layers of DPI, allowing for innovation on top. A critical challenge is securing sustainable funding for *maintenance*, not just building new things.

---

### **5. Closing Remarks**

*   **Roberto Di Cosmo (Software Heritage):** Emphasized that software development is a global undertaking with exponential growth. He made a call for building shared, non-profit, multi-stakeholder infrastructure to preserve, study, and understand our collective software commons, using hard data to drive policy.
*   **Final Message:** The day concluded by reinforcing the community's role as a driving force for solving global challenges. The passion and growth of the open-source movement are exponential, and its principles are essential for building a more equitable and transparent digital future.

# IDEAS

*   The best thing about open source is that everybody can contribute to its growth and development.
*   Simultaneously, the worst thing about open source is that absolutely everyone is able to contribute.
*   An organization should institutionalize open source practices so tangible results are seen year after year.
*   Naming a unit an OSPO helps newcomers identify a natural point of contact for collaboration.
*   A key argument for university OSPOs is linking open source directly to research and education missions.
*   Open source is a critical element in newer forms of university output like open hardware and AI.
*   Customer demand for more open source forwardness can be a powerful driver for OSPO creation.
*   OSPO motivation often begins with compliance but matures towards intentional community engagement and structured processes.
*   Civil society can be the critical external force that pushes a government to institutionalize open source.
*   Governments must prove open source is state-of-the-art when addressing the needs of civil servants.
*   The post-acquisition formalization of an OSPO can mature a company’s ad-hoc open source activities.
*   A national crisis of trust can be a powerful and unique driving force for government OSPOs.
*   The primary motivation for a government OSPO can be sovereignty, trust, and citizen-focused service delivery.
*   A government OSPO must hold a contested space for people with different needs in a commons.
*   Open source should be a foundational principle for government, not just an operational or cost-saving afterthought.
*   Changing an institution’s culture towards open source collaboration must be a pervasive, continuous, systematic effort.
*   You must depend on key people who know the ropes before you can pass that knowledge.
*   OSPOs risk becoming bureaucratic and must constantly prove their impact to justify long-term investment in maintenance.
*   An OSPO can be a tool to fight bureaucracy in a human way with a long-term view.
*   Governments fear losing control and taking on risk, preferring project thinking over product thinking models.
*   The digital execution gap in government is transforming into a much more severe trust crisis.
*   Digital sovereignty should not be confused with isolationism; it must equal open source and global cooperation.
*   The future of AI is not just about technology, but how we design and deploy it.
*   AI is not a zero-sum game; sharing research and code creates more value for everyone.
*   Open source AI democratizes access, allowing small businesses and startups to compete with larger corporations.
*   Our future interactions with the digital world will be mediated by AI systems we need to trust.
*   Software engineers spend a low percentage of their time building new software versus maintaining it.
*   AI agents will offload maintenance work, freeing up software engineers for next-generation innovation and creativity.
*   A major concern is the concentration of AI power in the hands of a few companies.
*   We do not need new values for AI; we need to protect the values we have.
*   Data is like uranium; used incorrectly it creates huge problems, but used correctly it’s powerful.
*   The friction of critical thinking is lost when an AI chatbot provides a single, synthesized answer.
*   Software is a primary output of a modern university, fostering research, education, and social impact.
*   The UN is not just a space for governments; it is a space for all stakeholders.
*   Humanity’s future AI systems will require collaboration from partners around the world to build them.

# INSIGHTS

*   An OSPO’s success depends on it becoming a cultural bridge, not just a technical or legal gatekeeper.
*   Open source is a governance model that invites scrutiny, accountability, and trust, not just a development methodology.
*   True digital sovereignty is achieved through open collaboration and shared standards, not isolationism and proprietary systems.
*   The greatest value of AI may be freeing human intellect from maintenance tasks to focus on innovation.
*   AI systems must reflect global diversity in language and culture, a task no single entity can accomplish.
*   Institutionalizing open source is about ensuring the excitement from events translates into tangible, sustainable, Monday-morning work.
*   Human rights frameworks are not obstacles to AI innovation but are the foundation that makes it possible.
*   The challenge is not creating new values for the digital age but upholding timeless human-centric principles.
*   Open data and open source are merging into a single conversation driven by the needs of AI.
*   Government digital transformation is no longer a technology problem but has become a governance and trust problem.
*   The open source movement is evolving from a technical community into a global multi-stakeholder governance ecosystem.
*   Real innovation is figuring out how to advance technologically without leaving a single person or community behind.
*   We must shift from viewing data as an asset to own to a commons to be stewarded.
*   Every decision, including inaction, is a governance decision that shapes our collective technological future and destiny.
*   Building capacity and practical implementation should often be prioritized over waiting for perfect governance frameworks to arrive.

# HABITS

*   Aligning an OSPO’s mission with the overarching strategic goals of its parent institution ensures relevance.
*   Starting an OSPO’s journey with a focus on legal compliance before expanding to proactive community engagement.
*   Building a community of internal champions and advocates to create exponential growth in open source adoption.
*   Viewing the OSPO as a bridge builder, translating between internal corporate language and external community norms.
*   Prototyping new ways of working in less contentious areas to demonstrate value and build organizational trust.
*   Mapping OSPO outcomes to the specific incentives and motivations of internal public servants and stakeholders.
*   Approaching open source not just as a technical choice but as a strategic foundation for trust.
*   Prioritizing the building of internal capacity over waiting for the arrival of a perfect governance model.
*   Applying the collaborative and transparent working models of open source internally, a practice called inner-sourcing.
*   Focusing on how technology solves a specific problem to attract a wider, more diverse talent pool.
*   Thinking of collaboration not as altruism but as your self-interest aligning with someone else’s self-interest.
*   Actively seeking out and learning from the journeys of other OSPOs to avoid common pitfalls.
*   Investing in documentation and training as you build your OSPO to scale knowledge and broaden impact.
*   Designing for trust and intentionally exposing the seams of a system instead of striving for seamlessness.
*   Centrally executing major digital budgets to drive fundamental change and break down long-standing bureaucratic silos.
*   Establishing open data basic guidelines that articulate principles of being open by default and machine readable.
*   Creating a pan-African data sharing community built on open source and open data principles as pillars.
*   Ensuring that AI tools being developed are always under the control of and in service to people.
*   Continuously investing in the maintenance of infrastructure, understanding it is as vital as creating new innovations.
*   Actively developing standards in the open to harness the expertise of an entire global expert community.
*   Focusing on creating high-quality test data to validate AI models, not just accumulating training data.

Humanity must collaboratively build open, transparent, and inclusive digital infrastructure to ensure technology serves everyone.

# RECOMMENDATIONS

*   Actively institutionalize open source through OSPOs to translate conference excitement into tangible, sustainable, real-world results.
*   Intentionally build a community of enthusiasts within your organization to find allies who are not so lonely.
*   Prioritize building internal digital capacity now instead of waiting for the perfect governance framework to be created.
*   Governments should seek alternatives to the geopolitical tech war by building shared, open source AI systems.
*   Democratize access to AI by releasing models as open source for everyone to use and improve.
*   Ensure future AI assistants speak all languages and understand all cultures through global open source collaboration.
*   Enable developers to understand the risks of AI and hold vendors accountable for their responsibilities.
*   Recognize that we do not need new values; we need to protect our existing human-centric values.
*   Focus on building vertical AI that solves specific, pressing issues rather than just creating another chatbot.
*   Procure open source solutions by focusing on projects with strong foundations and active communities of developers.
*   Think of collaboration as an alignment of self-interest, not just as a purely altruistic endeavor.
*   Remember that you are not alone on your OSPO journey; seek out established resources and networks.
*   Design for trust by intentionally revealing the seams of your systems, showing how things work.
*   Acknowledge that data is useless unless it is discoverable, accessible, and used with proper open standards.
*   Shift your cultural perspective on data from a model of ownership to one of active stewardship.
*   Move beyond just funding innovation and hackathons to providing continuous investment for long-term systems maintenance.
*   Public bodies should make sharing resources and code with each other a primary and essential task.
*   Work to make your country’s official statistical data AI-ready by adopting common international data standards.
*   Join emerging networks like the Public Sector OSPO Network to connect, communicate, and learn from peers.
*   Governments should look for commercial open source software that can be used across public and private sectors.
*   Adopt open source to build sovereignty and governance over information concerning your nation’s citizens.
*   Establish a global network of OSPOs across the Global South, learning from early adopters’ journeys.
