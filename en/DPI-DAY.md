### **Event Overview**

*   **Event:** DPI Day, part of the UN's Open Source Week.
*   **Host:** UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies.
*   **Co-Sponsors:** The governments of the Dominican Republic and Nigeria.
*   **Objective:** To discuss the implementation, challenges, and potential of Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) through keynote speeches, a panel on cross-border learning, and co-creative breakout sessions aimed at shaping the agenda for the Global DPI Summit in November.


### **1. Opening Remarks & Keynote Speeches**

The event was opened by **Moritz Carl** from the UN Office, who welcomed attendees and outlined the interactive program for the day. This was followed by a series of high-level keynote speeches.

#### **Amandeep Singh Gill - UN Under-Secretary-General and Special Envoy**
*   **Core Message:** DPI is a powerful, architected collaboration built on open-source principles that can achieve massive scale for public good without ruling out profit.
*   **Key Examples of DPI Success:**
    *   **India:** Financial inclusion soared from 35% to nearly 80% through Aadhaar and Jandhan.
    *   **Estonia:** e-Government saves 2% of GDP annually.
    *   **Brazil:** The PIX payments platform delivered over $5.7 billion in savings in its first year.
    *   **Kazakhstan:** A comprehensive digital family card system ensures no family in need is left behind.
*   **Critical Theme:** Trust is essential for DPI adoption. This requires building in safeguards and human rights protections "by design," not as an afterthought.
*   **Future Focus:** key next steps include ensuring interoperability, strengthening the interface between DPI and AI, and promoting experience sharing

#### **H.E. Minister Sigmund Freund - Dominican Republic**
*   **Core Message:** DPI is a test of values, designed to make the state more agile, accountable, and human. The Dominican Republic is choosing to build internal public sector capacity rather than relying on external consultants.
*   **Dominican Republic's Strategy:**
    1.  **Human Talent Development:** Building skills within the civil service.
    2.  **Government Reference Architecture:** Using DPI principles for interoperability and scalability.
    3.  **People-Centered Digital Services:** Secure, proactive, and accessible services.
*   **Key Initiatives:** Citing concrete examples like X-Road for interoperability and Soy yo RD, a user-controlled digital wallet.

#### **Vilas Dar - President, Patrick J. McGovern Foundation**
*   **Core Message:** There is no such thing as "neutral technology." Technology embeds values and decisions about power. Peace is no longer just about treaties; it is about building reliable infrastructure and systems that create trust.
*   **Call to Action for Technologists:** Reject the idea of building in isolation. Embrace multinational collaboration on open standards and shared governance.
*   **Vision:** DPI must be designed with intention to be just and humane. "Peace is a design choice."

#### **H.E. Dr. Bosun Tijani - Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, Nigeria**
*   **Core Message:** DPI offers a historic opportunity to rebuild trust between citizens and their governments, moving from a transactional state to a responsive one.
*   **DPI as a New Social Contract:** It's not just an evolution of e-government but a foundational layer for innovation and service delivery at scale.
*   **Nigeria's 5-Point Strategy:**
    1.  Investing in foundational infrastructure (connectivity, talent).
    2.  Establishing strong legal and governance frameworks.
    3.  Championing open standards and modular design.
    4.  Activating a vibrant DPI ecosystem for startups and innovators.
    5.  Maintaining a "moral clarity" that puts people at the center.

### **2. Panel: "Learning Across Borders"**

This panel explored global collaboration, challenges, and best practices in DPI implementation.

*   **Daniel Morenzi (East African Community):** Highlighted challenges like fragmented laws and low internet penetration, but also major successes like the rise of mobile money and real-time cross-border customs data exchange. Emphasized linking projects to universities to build local capacity.
*   **Yolanda Martinez (World Bank):** Stressed that success requires political support, institutional capacity, and digital skills. The World Bank is financing comprehensive digital operations and fostering knowledge sharing, such as a new consortium to build a regional DPI stack in Latin America.
*   **Tiffany Ferris (Drupal Foundation):** Drew lessons from the 25-year-old open-source Drupal project: the importance of transparent governance, investment in collaboration tools, and strong community norms for conflict resolution. Noted that sustainable funding for maintenance (not just new features) is a critical challenge for open-source projects.
*   **Mark Lepage (Asian Development Bank):** Focused on the financial challenges of DPI, including a lack of cost transparency, unsustainable funding models, and high ongoing operational costs. ADB is working with partners to address these issues through smart financing and capacity development.
*   **Sheila Warren (Project Liberty Institute):** Argued that DPI is a continuous *process*, not a finite *project*. It must be centered on citizen empowerment. She cited survey data showing deep public concern over data privacy, reinforcing the need to embed safeguards from the start and constantly adapt to user needs.

### **3. Audience Q&A Highlights**

Key questions from the audience focused on:
1.  **Local Capacity:** How to empower local technical providers to build and support DPI.
2.  **Citizen Trust:** How to overcome the perception that digital ID systems are politically motivated, especially around elections.
3.  **Scaling with Open Source:** How open-source tools can scale impact in sectors like health and education in low-resource settings.

The key objective was to synthesize discussions into actionable insights and calls to action for the upcoming Global DPI Summit.

### **4. Message from the International Telecommunications Union (ITU)**

A video message from **Thomas Lamanaskus, Deputy Secretary-General of the ITU**, highlighted the agency's commitment to open source as a vital enabler of DPI.

*   **Key Initiatives:**
    *   **GovStack:** Promotes open-source blueprints for modernizing public services.
    *   **Open Source Programme Offices (OSPOs):** A joint ITU-UNDP initiative helping governments (e.g., Kenya, Trinidad and Tobago) build institutional capacity for open source.
    *   **AI for Good:** An initiative to make AI a common good, with an upcoming summit in Geneva.
*   **Core Message:** The ITU practices what it preaches, having led the creation of the UN's own open-source policy framework. Open source allows nations to control their "own digital destiny" by reducing proprietary lock-in and investing in homegrown talent.

### **6. Panel: Empowering DPI Implementation Across Africa**

This panel explored the unique challenges and opportunities for DPI on the African continent.

*   **Keynote Remarks (Traveen Rabinath, South Africa G20 Presidency):** DPI is a top priority for the G20. Emphasized the need for "tailor-made solutions, by Africa, for Africa" to achieve technical sovereignty and avoid vendor lock-in.

*   **Panel Discussion:**
    *   **Unique African Context:** An audience poll and the panelists highlighted the continent's youth-led innovation, significant infrastructure constraints, profound digital inclusion challenges, and immense diversity (54 countries, ~3,000 cultures).
    *   **Key Challenges:** A lack of trust in both systems and local capacity; regulatory fragmentation; and a need for a common, human-centric understanding of what DPI aims to achieve.
    *   **Key Opportunities:** DPI can empower the youth and MSMEs, giving them access to credit and regional markets under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AFCFTA). Africa's lack of legacy systems in some areas provides a unique chance to build interoperable systems "right from the start."
    *   **Most Critical Need:** Panelists unanimously agreed that **capacity building** and **community engagement** are the two areas requiring the most urgent and sustained support.

# IDEAS

*   Peace is no longer just a matter of treaties but of reliable, trust-building digital infrastructure.
*   Technology and technologists are never neutral; they embed cultural values and power decisions into products.
*   Digital Public Infrastructure should be viewed as a new form of social contract to rebuild trust.
*   Governments should prioritize building internal capability instead of reflexively outsourcing core functions to management consultants.
*   Human rights safeguards must be integrated into Digital Public Infrastructure by design, not as an afterthought.
*   The paradox of public interest technology is that it can serve the public good while not ruling out profit.
*   DPI must be understood as a continuous process without a defined endpoint, not a series of projects.
*   Digital sovereignty means making a conscious choice about what to control, not building everything yourself.
*   Public services should be architected around citizens' life events, not siloed government agency structures.
*   Open source communities need norms not just for working but for how to decide when they disagree.
*   The state can be a quiet, intelligent, and enabling force in citizens’ lives, not an intruder.
*   Maintaining mature open source projects is like ocean swimming: requiring immense effort just to stay still.
*   DPI can unlock significant intra-regional trade in places like Africa, boosting continent-wide economic growth.
*   We must reject the idea that innovators should be left alone without multinational guidance or regulation.
*   The most successful innovators cultivate five core skills: associating, questioning, observing, experimenting, and networking.
*   A "digital-first" approach is essential for modern services, but it must not become a "digital-only" approach.
*   Even with good constitutional protections, flawed system design can still end up institutionalizing societal harm.
*   Shared governance models should treat technology as co-stewarded infrastructure, not a product for profitable export.
*   AI systems are meaningfully shaped by data and model inputs far from the problem’s proximity.
*   University students can build national projects, creating local content and supporting long-term sovereign technical capacity.
*   A key challenge for governments is moving beyond prizing presentation skills to valuing measurable delivery.
*   Open source contribution tracking systems should recognize both code and non-code contributions to incentivize participation.
*   The complexity and fragmentation of financing models is one of the biggest obstacles to DPI adoption.
*   Operational costs for DPI often exceed the initial capital costs, requiring sustainable long-term funding models.
*   Technology can be used to move governments from a transactional state to a truly responsive state.
*   We need to stop treating open source as simple software procurement and start building communities.
*   For DPI to succeed, we must create an observatory of emerging risks to proactively adapt.
*   The local context and unique needs of a country should be balanced with common, shared technologies.

# INSIGHTS

*   Technology is fundamentally reshaping governance, with DPI becoming the infrastructure for a new social contract.
*   True digital sovereignty is achieved through strategic autonomy and conscious choices, not technological isolationism.
*   The success of any digital system depends on its social infrastructure: community, trust, and shared norms.
*   Human-centric design is the core principle for building trusted digital systems that truly empower citizens.
*   DPI is a powerful engine for inclusive economic development, creating jobs and unlocking regional trade.
*   Building technology is an inherently political and ethical act that requires intentional design for human values.
*   Public services must be reimagined around the human experience, breaking free from bureaucratic government silos.
*   Sustainable financial and governance models for digital public goods are critical for their long-term survival.
*   Cultivating local talent through partnerships is the only way to achieve sustainable, long-term digital independence.
*   Technical interoperability offers a practical path to global cooperation while respecting national regulatory diversity.
*   Lasting digital transformation is built from within by public servants who are curious and committed.
*   The move from potential to reality in Africa's digital economy requires inclusive and locally owned DPI.

# HABITS

*   Intentionally embedding innovator skills like questioning, observing, and experimenting into public sector training academies.
*   Actively cultivating community norms for both how to work and how to resolve disagreements respectfully.
*   Adopting a "digital-first, but not digital-only" approach in all service designs to ensure inclusion.
*   Continuously asking who is in the design process and who is not, to proactively address gaps.
*   Designing citizen services around key life events, not around existing and siloed government structures.
*   Using university students and local academic partnerships to build national projects and foster homegrown talent.
*   Making a conscious, strategic decision about which technologies to build locally versus which to adopt.
*   Placing outcome metrics and real user satisfaction ahead of slide decks and speeches in evaluation.
*   Building systems to be transparent, participatory, and just in order to systematically earn public trust.
*   Integrating human-rights safeguards into technology from the beginning, rather than adding them on later.
*   Actively sharing frameworks, technical documentation, and lessons learned with other countries to accelerate progress.
*   Establishing public recognition systems for both code and non-code contributions to incentivize community participation.
*   Developing proactive and agile communication strategies to address emerging risks in evolving DPI systems.
*   Treating DPI as an ever-evolving process that does not have a defined, static endpoint.
*   Thinking locally to solve immediate problems while also thinking regionally and globally for broader impact.
*   Creating consortiums with partners to build regional infrastructure like a shared digital ID broker.
*   Building technology solutions that respond to identified needs, not inventing problems to fit a solution.
*   Planning for the full DPI lifecycle, including growth and evolution, not just initial investment.
*   Using a step-wise implementation approach with pilots to learn and adapt before any full-scale rollouts.
*   Creating a DPI ecosystem that enables startups and civic tech to build on foundational rails.
*   Bringing oppositional parties into the same room to facilitate consensus on multi-stakeholder technology projects.

# ONE-SENTENCE TAKEAWAY

Building human-centric, trusted digital infrastructure requires intentional design, open collaboration, and sovereign local ownership.

# RECOMMENDATIONS

*   Invest in your own civil servants and internal capabilities instead of outsourcing to management consultants.
*   Build human rights safeguards directly into all Digital Public Infrastructure systems from the very beginning.
*   Treat DPI as a continuous process without a defined end, constantly adapting to evolving contexts.
*   Create national DPI frameworks that unite all related initiatives under one coherent strategic agenda.
*   Leverage local innovation ecosystems by providing clear incentives for working with local technology companies.
*   Ensure digital sovereignty by making conscious choices about which technologies to control and which to adopt.
*   Develop proactive, agile communication strategies for DPI to engage stakeholders and address emerging risks.
*   Establish clear community norms not just for collaboration but for respectfully resolving disagreements and conflicts.
*   Implement a "digital-first" but never a "digital-only" approach to service delivery to guarantee inclusion.
*   Focus on measurable improvements in people's lives, prioritizing tangible outcomes over polished slide decks.
*   Pool regional resources and expertise to share the labor of deploying and maintaining DPI components.
*   Support the development of a private ecosystem for open source tools to ensure their sustainability.
*   Plan for the full lifecycle of DPI, including maintenance, support, and continuous technological evolution.
*   Co-create digital systems with the people they are meant to serve, not just for them.
*   Harmonize technical standards and APIs across regions to facilitate seamless cross-border data and services.
*   Create joint capacity development programs that include government, regulators, and the private sector together.
*   Governments must invest in people and local developer capacity, not just in finished software products.
*   Revise old laws, some potentially centuries old, to enable modern digital data sharing practices.
*   Establish robust, well-resourced oversight bodies that can enforce data protection and build user trust.
*   Design all citizen services around key life events, not around existing bureaucratic government silos.
*   Translate technical documentation and resources to make them accessible to other countries and partners.
*   Come up with procurement innovation tools to mitigate legal risks for acquiring open source solutions.
*   Use a stepwise implementation approach, learning from initial pilots before attempting full-scale national rollouts.
